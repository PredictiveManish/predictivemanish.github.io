On September 15, 2026, a company called TypeSafe launched Jev — a “System 1” decision model. You hand it a state (an email, a ticket, a JSON document) plus typed questions, and it returns choices, scores and calibrated probabilities instead of paragraphs. Three days later, Convai Innovations released Laya: the same idea, but open weights, Apache 2.0, 421 million parameters, installable with pip install laya.

Every latency figure in that debate since — the 38 ms headlines, the “faster than Jev” claims — comes from Tesla T4s, RTX 5090s and Mac M2s. Hardware most people will never touch. Nobody asked the obvious question: what happens on the laptop most of us actually own?

So I ran it on mine: an Acer Aspire 7 with an i5-12450H, 16 GB of RAM and a GTX 1650 with 4 GB of VRAM. A 2019-class gaming laptop — the kind a student buys, not the kind a lab benchmarks on.

Methodology

What I actually measured

I wrote a small benchmark harness (published, with raw JSON output) that measures cold load time and warm latency for 1, 10 and 50 typed questions answered in a single call — the three scenarios the model card publishes T4 numbers for. Each scenario ran 100 timed calls after 5 warmup calls, reporting p50, p95 and mean.

Two details matter more than they sound. First, torch.cuda.synchronize() fires before every timer stops — CUDA calls are asynchronous, and without the sync you time the launch, not the work. Second, every timed call receives a different input email, so no pass ever sees byte-identical input. My environment is in the repo, because a latency number without its environment is a vibe, not a measurement.

Read it plainly: a 2019 consumer laptop is 15–22× slower than the published T4 figures. Every single-question decision still completes in under a second — not fast, but real, local, free and offline. Cold load took 29.7 s and 57.1 s on two consecutive runs from a warm cache: “time to first decision” on a laptop is a different story from a warm server.

One number surprised me in the good direction: batching still amortizes on weak hardware.

Per-question cost, by number of questions answered in one forward pass (my p50 values).

Per-question cost drops from 847 ms to 224 ms — a 3.8× reduction. The “one forward pass for everything” design argument, which is Laya’s whole pitch against paying per API call, holds even on a GTX 1650. It is just uniformly slower.

The anomaly

The thing the T4 tables can’t show you

On the 50-question scenario, my mean (26.5 s) is 2.4× my own median (11.2 s) — and above my p95 (11.9 s). That is not noise; that is a signature. A small number of runs took minutes instead of seconds.

Working hypothesis

The 50-question batch briefly needs more memory than the GTX 1650’s 4 GB, and Windows spills into “shared GPU memory” — system RAM reached over the PCIe bus, which is brutally slow. Thermal throttling cannot produce a mean above the p95; memory spillover can. Stated as a hypothesis until I instrument GPU memory to confirm it.

If you are deploying anything like this on 4 GB consumer cards: batch smaller, or verify your headroom first. Nobody publishing T4 numbers will ever tell you where the ceiling is, because their ceiling is nowhere in sight.

Smoke test

Does it get decisions right?

Latency is only half the story. I also ran ten hand-labeled emails — billing complaints, phishing attempts, HR notices, bug reports, a lottery scam — and asked the model three questions about each.

Phishing was the standout: the single miss was a P=0.60 shrug, not a confident error, and the separation between scams and legitimate mail was decisive. Department routing was the interesting failure: every miss was a scam or complaint email absorbed into “billing” or “technical” — because my answer space had no security option. The model reads your option descriptions and routes into the best semantic overlap. One of my own labels was arguably wrong too.

With this class of model, your question schema is part of the model. Design the answer space badly and you measure your own schema, not the model.

To be clear about what this is not: ten emails is a smoke test, not an accuracy benchmark. The model’s own card says base Laya scores near chance zero-shot on decision benchmarks — the intended workflow is fine-tuning on your own data.

Fine print

Honest caveats

  • The T4 column is the model card’s published figure. I did not measure it.
  • The Jev numbers circulating in this debate are third-party, from different prompts and sample sizes. Every cross-model comparison — including the vendors’ — is indicative at best.
  • The spillover explanation for the mean anomaly is my best diagnosis, stated as a hypothesis until instrumented with GPU memory telemetry.
  • One machine, one session, fp32, plugged into power, no GPU-utilization telemetry for every run.

Takeaway

Why this number matters

On a 2019 gaming laptop, the open decision model answers any single typed question in under a second — free, offline, on your own weights. The cloud one is faster and bills per call. Neither number tells you which is right for you; but only one of them runs when the wifi is down, and only one of them you can fine-tune on a free Kaggle GPU and put back in the same folder as your college notes.

The gap between the benchmark world and the real world is 15–22×, measured. It is not a reason to stay off this wave. It is just the number nobody had written down.
